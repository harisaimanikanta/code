Perth, Australia. The early nineteen eighties. Medicine had a rule: nothing can live in the acid of the stomach.

But pathologist Robin Warren kept seeing something in his biopsies. Spiral bacteria, living in the stomach lining.

A young doctor, Barry Marshall, believed him. Together they presented their findings. They were rejected.

They tried to infect pigs. The pigs stayed healthy.

So Marshall did the unthinkable. He drank the bacteria himself. Within days, he was violently ill.

An endoscope showed his stomach inflamed, and swarming with bacteria. The bacteria survive by neutralising the acid around them. And antibiotics could wipe them out.

When the case was published, the patient was him.

In 2005, Warren and Marshall received the Nobel Prize.

Sometimes, the proof has to go through you.
