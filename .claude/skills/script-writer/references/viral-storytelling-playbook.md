## 1. Hook patterns

### What the titles show [OBSERVED - 64 titles, `research/competitor-database.md`]
| Title pattern | Count | Share | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| LABEL (event name ± adjective) | 19 | 30% | "The Great Molasses Flood of 1919" - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/HJ8YSh46Oy4 |
| PLAIN-CLAIM (state the absurd fact) | 11 | 17% | "They Fought a War Over a Bucket!" - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/VR_sZ_NUaR4 |
| PERSON-WHO ("The man who…") | 11 | 17% | "The Man Who Sold the Eiffel Tower (Twice)" - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/q5hqKfX-OE4 |
| CONTRADICTION | 8 | 13% | "WWII Was Over but This Soldier Kept Fighting for 29 Years" - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/RaklWFblRIQ |
| SUPERLATIVE | 6 | 9% | "Australia's CRAZIEST War…" - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/wGcI9NBfj1w |
| QUESTION (how/why) | 4 | 6% | "The Great Emu War: Why Australia Lost" - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/xrtQppbv-uI |
| REACTION ("This actually happened") | 3 | 5% | https://www.youtube.com/shorts/wTGj7XRQAHQ |
| CAUSE-EFFECT (tiny cause → huge result) | 2 | 3% | "How a Stolen Well Bucket Sparked a Bloody Battle…" - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/u4mtQZNA6KA |

Other title facts [OBSERVED]: 30/64 contain a number (year, count, duration); 11/64 use an ALL-CAPS word; 6/64 put hashtags in the title;
average title length ≈ 50 characters. Exact duplicate titles occur (3× "Australia's Bizarre Battle: The Great Emu War",
3× "Boston's Deadly Molasses Flood: 1919 Disaster").

**Takeaway [ASSUMPTION]:** the most common pattern (LABEL) is also the least curious - it names the event without
saying what's absurd about it. The space is crowded with near-identical titles on the same ~5 famous stories, so a
title that states the *specific absurd element* (PLAIN-CLAIM / CONTRADICTION / CAUSE-EFFECT) is the differentiator to test.
Performance data is needed before calling any pattern "best-performing".

### Hook types to use in the voiceover [ASSUMPTION + EXTERNAL]
Third-party Shorts guides (vidIQ, others) put the scroll decision at roughly the first 1–3 s and recommend
leading with the payoff-promise rather than intros [EXTERNAL: https://vidiq.com/blog/post/viral-video-hooks-youtube-shorts/].
For this niche, in priority order:
1. **Impossible fact, stated flat** - "In 1925, a man sold the Eiffel Tower." The fact *is* the hook.
2. **Contradiction** - two true things that can't both be true.
3. **Tiny cause → enormous effect** - a bucket, a pig, an emu.
4. **In medias res** - drop into the decisive scene with a time/place stamp.
5. **Mystery** - only when the mystery is genuine (unknown identity, unsolved cause).
Avoid: "Did you know", "This is the craziest…", generic REACTION openers - they promise without delivering a fact.

## 2. Storytelling structures [ASSUMPTION]
- **The Con Arc** (cons, hoaxes, impostors): impossible result → how the mark was set up → the one detail that made it believable → the escape / exposure → the ironic aftermath.
- **The Absurd Escalation** (wars, disasters): ridiculous trigger → it gets taken seriously → it escalates beyond reason → body count / cost → the absurd footnote.
- **The Long Fuse** (Onoda, Piltdown): something false is believed → years pass → the moment of discovery → what it cost.
- **The Trial / Verdict** (Cadaver Synod, animal trials): the absurd proceeding → the procedure taken seriously → the verdict → the backlash.

## 3. Narration patterns [ASSUMPTION - verify with transcripts]
- Conversational, second-person-adjacent ("Picture this" sparingly), present tense for scenes, past tense for facts.
- Sentences 4–14 words; a long build sentence followed by a short punch.
- Numbers spoken concretely; years as time-stamps, not lectures.
- Personality comes from *selection* (which detail you choose) and dry understatement, not from adjectives.

## 4. Retention techniques [ASSUMPTION + EXTERNAL]
- Name the central absurd event in the first sentence; withhold the *mechanism* and the *outcome*.
- New information every 3–5 s; every sentence answers one question and raises the next.
- One genuine mid-story turn ("But one dealer went to the police") around 40–60% of runtime.
- Biggest reveal in the back third; final line recontextualizes the hook.
- External guides describe diagnosing a sharp drop in the first ~2 s as a hook problem and a later drop as a payoff problem
  [EXTERNAL: https://www.tubeanalytics.net/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-guide]. Use your own Studio retention graphs to test this.

## 5. Curiosity-gap techniques [ASSUMPTION]
- Open loops with a *specific* promise ("There was one problem: the tower wasn't for sale.").
- "Why didn't anyone report it?" - plant the question, answer it 15 s later.
- Withhold a number, then land it.
- Never open a loop you don't close.

## 6. Emotional triggers [ASSUMPTION]
Disbelief ("this can't be real") · admiration for audacity · schadenfreude at fooled elites · dread (dark stories) ·
absurd humour · the "smarter than everyone" feeling of knowing the real version behind the myth.

## 7. Ending strategies [ASSUMPTION]
- **Ironic epitaph** - the final document/detail that mocks the story (a con man's death certificate calling him a salesman).
- **Scale-flip** - zoom out to how big the consequence really was.
- **Myth correction** - "The famous version says X. The record says Y - which is somehow stranger."
- **Loop** - last line echoes the first so a rewatch feels seamless.
No "like and subscribe". If a CTA is used, make it a genuine question that fits the story (pinned comment).

## 8. Visual storytelling patterns [ASSUMPTION - Round 1 had no visual access]
Archival photo/clipping first; labelled AI reconstruction for undocumented scenes; maps for geography;
new visual every 1.5–3 s; burned-in captions. See `references/visual-storytelling-guidelines.md`.

## 9. Common mistakes [OBSERVED in titles / ASSUMPTION for content]
- [OBSERVED] Generic LABEL titles and verbatim duplicate titles across uploads.
- [OBSERVED] Agenda-driven reframing of historical hoaxes (e.g. Piltdown used to argue against evolution - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/SsraBFNzphg excluded from our dataset for that reason). Don't do this.
- [OBSERVED] Unqualified legend numbers in titles ("400 People Danced Until They DIED" - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/cquvAN3evSQ); the death toll is disputed by historians.
- [OBSERVED] "Sold it twice" presented as fact in many Lustig titles; the second attempt is reported as failing (see `research/scripts/eiffel-tower-lustig/research.md`).
- [ASSUMPTION] Slow context openings, lists of facts instead of a story, ending on "and that's the story of…".

## 10. Recommended framework for this channel - "The Paper Trail"
Original identity to differentiate from the crowded "crazy true story" field:
1. **Hook (0–3 s):** the impossible fact, flat and specific.
2. **Stamp (3–8 s):** place + year on screen; one line of who.
3. **Mechanism (8–35 s):** how it actually worked - the clever, specific details competitors skip.
4. **Turn (35–50 s):** the moment it broke (or didn't).
5. **The Paper Trail (last 8–15 s):** the document, record or detail that proves it - or that the famous version is wrong.
   Ends on irony. On screen: the source type ("1961 memoir of the Secret Service agent who caught him").

Why: accuracy becomes the brand ("we show the receipt"), myth-corrections give an ending competitors can't copy,
and it rewards viewers who know the famous version. Hypothesis - track it against retention data.
