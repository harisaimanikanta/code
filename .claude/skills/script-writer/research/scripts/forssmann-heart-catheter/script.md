## C. Final voiceover script (Draft 4)
<!-- VOICEOVER START -->
How did a doctor push a tube into his own heart, and survive?
In nineteen twenty-nine, doctors believed touching the inside of a heart meant death.
So when Werner Forssmann, just twenty-five, asked to try it, his boss said no.
He did it anyway. But he needed tools, and the nurse who kept them had one rule. Try it on her first.
According to his own memoir, he strapped her to the table and numbed her arm.
Then, while she waited, he quietly cut open his own.
He slid a thin, bendy tube into a vein at his elbow. Slowly, he pushed. More than half a metre.
There was one problem. He couldn't see where the tube had gone.
So, with it still inside him, he walked to the X-ray room.
The picture came out. The tip of the tube was sitting inside his heart.
And he was still alive.
He thought he'd be a hero. Instead, a famous surgeon reportedly called it a circus stunt.
Years later, he wasn't even a heart doctor. He treated bladders in a small town.
Then, twenty-seven years after that walk to the X-ray room, he won the Nobel Prize.
Today, doctors slide tubes into hearts every single day.
Because the secret was simple. Every vein in your arm is a road that leads back to your heart.
He didn't break into his heart. He followed the road home.
<!-- VOICEOVER END -->

### Draft 1
Why would a young doctor push a tube into his own heart?
It's nineteen twenty-nine, in a small German hospital. Back then, when a heart stopped, doctors stabbed a long needle through the chest, straight into the heart. It was dangerous.
Werner Forssmann, just twenty-five, had a crazy idea. What if medicine could ride a tube into the heart instead?
His boss said no. Doctors believed touching the inside of a heart would kill you.
So he did it anyway, on himself.
He numbed his own arm. He cut it open. Then he slid a thin, bendy tube into a vein.
Inch by inch, he pushed. More than half a metre.
But he couldn't see where it went. So with the tube still inside him, he walked to the X-ray room.
The picture came out. The tip of the tube was sitting inside his heart. And he was alive.
Here's the trick. Your veins are one-way roads. Every vein in your arm flows back to the heart. So a soft tube doesn't need to break anything. It just follows the road home.
He thought he'd be a hero. Instead, a famous surgeon called it a circus trick. Forssmann quit heart medicine and became a small-town doctor.
Then, twenty-seven years later, he won the Nobel Prize.
Today, doctors use his road every day to save hearts.
