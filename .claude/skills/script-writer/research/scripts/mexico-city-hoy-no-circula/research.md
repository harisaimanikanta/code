# Research: Why Banning Cars Put More Cars on the Road (Hoy No Circula)

- **Slug:** mexico-city-hoy-no-circula · **Researched:** 2026-11-19 · **Verdict:** GO WITH QUALIFIERS
- **Historical period:** 1989-2008 · **Place:** Mexico City
- **Main people:** Mexico City government (program owner); Gunnar Eskeland & Tarhan Feyzioglu (World Bank economists, 1997 study); Lucas W. Davis (economist, 2008 study)

## Short factual summary
On 20 November 1989 Mexico City introduced Hoy No Circula ("Today [it] doesn't drive"), banning most cars from driving one weekday (5 a.m.-10 p.m.) based on the last digit of the licence plate, about 20% of cars per weekday. Announced as a temporary winter measure, it was made permanent in 1990. Two independent economic studies found it did not deliver: Eskeland & Feyzioglu (World Bank Economic Review, 1997, "Rationing Can Backfire") found total driving rose, partly because some households bought an additional car; Davis (Journal of Political Economy, 2008) found no evidence of improved air quality across pollutants, plus increases in vehicle registrations and a shift toward older, higher-emitting cars.

## Evidence buckets
### VERIFIED FACTS
- Start date 20 Nov 1989; ban by last plate digit; one weekday; ~20% of cars per weekday - S2, S4
- Made permanent in 1990 - S4, S5
- Davis 2008: no evidence of improved air quality across pollutants and specifications, using hourly monitoring-station data - S2, S3
- Davis 2008: evidence of more vehicles in circulation and a shift toward high-emitting vehicles; more registrations - S2 (abstract), S3
- Eskeland & Feyzioglu 1997: total driving increased; some households bought an additional car; old cars, weekend driving contributed - S1
### REPORTED CLAIMS (attribute)
- Slight rise in gasoline sales after the ban - secondary summaries of Davis 2008 (S8). Script: "fuel sales don't fall".
- UN called Mexico City the world's most polluted city in 1992 - widely reported (S6), primary document not seen. Script avoids it.
### HISTORICAL DISPUTES
- How many households bought a second car: a review finds weak direct evidence for the "second-car hypothesis"; one survey summary says under 5% bought another car (S9). Script says "some families", never "everyone".
- Davis sample period (1986-2005 vs 1986-1993 for parts of the analysis). Script says "years of hourly readings".
- Whether permanence was announced in March 1990 or at season's end. Script says "By 1990".
- Second-car purchases: supported by 1994 contemporary reporting (Christian Science Monitor, citing studies incl. one by INAINE: the program encouraged people to buy a second, often older and more polluting car) and Eskeland & Feyzioglu 1997; but later household studies (Transportation Research Part D, 2017 and 2022) do not find second-car buying to be a main adaptation, and one textbook summary says most owners simply stopped using their car on the restricted day. Script keeps "some families".
### POPULAR LEGENDS (don't state as fact)
- Birds dropping dead mid-flight from smog - anecdotal; omit.
### UNKNOWN
- Exact number of extra cars attributable to the ban.

## Script-safety notes
- Plain fact: the dates, the plate rule, "one car in five", permanence by 1990, "no evidence the air got cleaner" (Davis), registrations up, older/dirtier mix, "some households bought another car" (Eskeland & Feyzioglu).
- Attribute: the economist's finding (name Davis); fuel sales.
- Avoid: "everyone bought a second car", "made the air worse", UN ranking, bird deaths, exact car counts.
- No characters: a search for a real, named driver who bought a second car (1990s press) found none, so the script uses no individual people except economist Lucas Davis. The v4 composite driver and wife were dropped at the owner's request.
