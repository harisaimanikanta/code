# Why Banning Cars Put More Cars on the Road - Shorts Package

Format: **True Mechanism Parable** · 2026-11-19 · From IDEA CARD (idea-creator batch 2026-11-19-mixed-2 #9) · Research: `research.md` (GO WITH QUALIFIERS)

## A. Story research
- **Period:** 1989-2008 · **Place:** Mexico City · **Characters:** the city government; economists Eskeland & Feyzioglu (1997) and Lucas Davis (2008)
- **Summary:** From 20 Nov 1989, each car was banned from driving one weekday by the last digit of its plate. Made permanent in 1990. Two peer-reviewed studies found no air-quality gain and evidence of more driving, more registrations and an older, dirtier fleet, partly because some households bought an extra car.
- **Verified:** dates, plate rule, one-in-five, permanence, Davis's no-improvement finding, registrations/older fleet, "some households bought an additional car".
- **Attributed:** fuel sales didn't fall. **Disputed:** how many families bought a second car (script says "some"). **Avoided:** UN ranking, dead birds, "made the air worse".
- **Sources:** World Bank Economic Review 1997, Journal of Political Economy 2008, Scientific Reports 2017, RFF, Chilango, Wikipedia (leads).

## C. Final voiceover script (218 words · ~62 s at 3.5 words/s)
<!-- VOICEOVER START -->
A city banned every car for one day a week, and ended up with more cars.
Sounds backwards, right?
You see, it's November 1989 in Mexico City, one of the smoggiest cities on Earth. The government tries something simple. Hoy No Circula. Today, you don't drive.
The rule is the last digit of your licence plate. Ends in five or six? No driving on Mondays. Seven or eight? Tuesdays.
Every weekday, one car in five should vanish.
It's supposed to be temporary. By 1990, it's permanent.
But the smog doesn't lift.
So economists start counting.
First, fuel. Sales don't fall.
Then the registration offices. More cars, not fewer.
Then the cars themselves. Older. Dirtier.
Years later, economist Lucas Davis checks the city's pollution monitors, hour by hour. He finds no evidence the ban cleaned the air at all.
So where did the extra cars come from?
The ban doesn't stop a driver. It stops a plate.
If your plate ends in a five, you just need one that ends in a seven.
So some families buy a second car. Usually a cheap, old one, the kind that pollutes the most.
Now they can drive every day of the week.
The ban was meant to take one car in five off the road.
In some driveways, it added one.
<!-- VOICEOVER END -->

## E. Editing direction
- **Pace:** a cut every ~1.2 s; hold 1.5 s on "But the smog doesn't lift." and on "So where did the extra cars come from?"
- **SFX:** stamp THUD at the open (it returns as registration stamps); petrol-pump ding; backfire BANG on "Older. Dirtier."; flatline tone on "no evidence"; one horn honk at the end.
- **Music:** ticking percussion for the rules → pizzicato "investigation" bed for the economists → drop to silence before the mechanism → playful bassoon for the second car → dead stop.
- **Captions:** 1–2 words, bold white, lower-middle. Extra text only: "MEXICO CITY · 1989", "1 IN 5", "1990", "FUEL ↑", "CARS ↑", "NO IMPROVEMENT".
- **Taste/accuracy:** the driver is a composite; tag "Dramatization". Don't show a "most polluted city" headline (unverified primary). Don't show the air getting *worse*.
- **Loop:** the final street shot mirrors the opening street shot, with doubled cars.
