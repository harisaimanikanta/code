Title: Why Old Poison Bottles Were Covered in Bumps 😳
Category: other (hidden reason)
Era & place: 1850s-1930s, Britain / USA
Cold open: "If you grabbed a bottle in the dark, how would you know it was poison?"
True story: Victorian poison bottles were made in cobalt blue or green glass with ridges, ribs, diamond patterns or embossed "POISON" so they could be told apart from medicine. In 1894 Frederick Rosher patented bottles "indicating by touch or sight" that they held poison.
Doubt beat: People took medicine by candlelight, and labels were easily misread or unreadable to people who couldn't read.
Reversal: The glass itself became the warning label - your fingers could read it.
Mechanism: Medicine bottles were smooth; poison bottles had raised ridges, so touch alone told them apart, even in the dark.
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (no on-topic Shorts found; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co113554/blue-ridged-glass-bottle-for-arsenic-europe-1701-1935-bottle · https://www.iflscience.com/the-deadly-reason-some-old-glass-bottles-are-textured-71268 · Wellcome (Rosher 1894 patent) https://iiif.wellcomecollection.org/pdf/b30736006
Watch out: "AMA recommended it in 1872" is unverified - skip. Not every poison bottle was textured.
Fresh angle: Payoff - it was braille for poison.

Title: Why Soldiers Stop Marching on Bridges 🤔
Category: engineering
Era & place: 12 April 1831, Broughton (Salford), England
Cold open: "When soldiers reach a bridge, they're ordered to stop marching in step."
True story: Soldiers marching in step across the Broughton Suspension Bridge set it vibrating, and it collapsed into the River Irwell. Afterwards the British Army ordered troops to "break step" on bridges.
Doubt beat: A few dozen men weigh far less than the carts the bridge carried every day.
Reversal: It wasn't the weight - it was the rhythm.
Mechanism: Each synchronised step pushes the bridge at the same moment; if that matches the bridge's natural sway, each push adds to the last (resonance).
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: medium (2-3 Shorts, e.g. "How Marching Soldiers Destroyed a Bridge"; estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broughton_Suspension_Bridge · https://www.livescience.com/34608-break-stride-frequency-of-vibration.html · https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Broughton_Suspension_Bridge
Watch out: Soldier count (60-74) and injuries vary; a failed bolt was also blamed. Say "helped bring down", not "single cause". No deaths.
Fresh angle: Visual - swap the marching soldiers for a crowd jumping on a modern bridge (Millennium Bridge 2000 wobble, if verified separately).

Title: Why Coins Have Ridges on the Edge 🤔
Category: con (prevention)
Era & place: 1696-1698, Royal Mint, London
Cold open: "People used to shave the edges off silver coins and spend them anyway."
True story: Hand-struck silver coins had irregular edges, so "clippers" shaved silver off and passed the coin at face value. During the Great Recoinage, with Isaac Newton at the Mint (warden from 1696), new machine-made coins with milled edges made clipping obvious.
Doubt beat: A tiny sliver from each coin seemed harmless - but across millions it debased the currency.
Reversal: The man behind gravity spent his days chasing coin clippers and counterfeiters.
Mechanism: A shaved coin loses its ridges, so anyone can feel the tampering instantly.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reeding · https://perthmint.com/news/collector/coin-collecting/how-isaac-newton-helped-shape-our-coins · https://www.mentalfloss.com/live-smarter/money/why-coins-have-ridges
Watch out: Newton didn't invent reeding (earlier Spanish coins had it). Don't say he "invented" ridges.
Fresh angle: Newton as coin cop.

Title: Why Ships Have This Weird Symbol 🚢
Category: backfired plan (insurance fraud)
Era & place: 1860s-1876, Britain
Cold open: "Some ship owners made more money when their ships sank."
True story: "Coffin ships" were overloaded and over-insured, so a sinking could pay the owner more than a safe voyage. MP Samuel Plimsoll campaigned until the 1876 Merchant Shipping Act required a load line on hulls.
Doubt beat: Plimsoll was fought by shipowners in Parliament for years.
Reversal: The circle-and-line on every cargo ship today is named after him.
Mechanism: The line shows how low the hull may sit in the water; if it's under the surface, the ship is overloaded - visible from the dock.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: low (explainer Shorts on load lines exist, none on coffin ships; estimated)
Evidence: https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/topics/samuel-plimsoll-ship-safety · https://www.britannica.com/biography/Samuel-Plimsoll
Watch out: Until 1894 owners chose where to paint the line. Don't claim the 1876 Act fixed it instantly.

Title: Why Romans Washed Clothes in Pee 🤢
Category: other (gross history)
Era & place: 1st century AD, Rome
Cold open: "Roman laundry workers stomped your toga in a tub of stale urine."
True story: Fullers (fullones) collected urine from pots and latrines and trod clothes in it to clean and brighten them. Emperor Vespasian taxed urine collection; when his son objected, Vespasian held a coin to his nose: "pecunia non olet" (money doesn't smell).
Doubt beat: His son Titus found the tax disgusting.
Reversal: The phrase is still used 2,000 years later.
Mechanism: Urea in stale urine breaks down into ammonia, an alkali that dissolves grease - the same ingredient as in cleaning sprays.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (estimated)
Evidence: https://www.worldhistory.org/article/46/the-fullers-of-rome/ · Suetonius, Vespasian 23 · https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Fullo.html
Watch out: Exactly who paid the tax is uncertain. Don't claim Romans whitened teeth with urine (literary source only).

Title: Why Victorian Wallpaper Could Kill You 😧
Category: medical
Era & place: 1850s-1860s, Britain
Cold open: "In 1862, four children fell ill and died in the same bright green bedroom."
True story: Scheele's green, a copper-arsenite pigment, made fashionable green wallpaper. In 1862 four Limehouse children died; the medical officer blamed the arsenic in their wallpaper, which tested at ~3 grains of arsenic per square foot.
Doubt beat: Manufacturers (including William Morris, whose family owned an arsenic mine) dismissed the danger.
Reversal: The most fashionable colour in Britain was poison on the walls.
Mechanism: Arsenic dust flaked off into the air; damp and mould may also have released arsenic gas.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 2)
Competition: low (estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheele%27s_green · https://jvc.oup.com/2023/12/14/arsenic-and-old-wallpapers/ · https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/conservation-blog/weeks-post-free-arsenic
Watch out: The arsine-gas mechanism is suspected, not proven - say "may". The Napoleon wallpaper theory is speculative; avoid. Verify the Morris arsenic-mine quote separately.

Title: Why Hangmen Weighed Prisoners First 😧
Category: other (hidden reason, dark)
Era & place: 1870s-1964, Britain
Cold open: "Before every execution, the hangman quietly checked the prisoner's weight."
True story: Hangman William Marwood popularised the "long drop" in the 1870s; an official Home Office Table of Drops (1888, revised 1892 and 1913) set the drop length by body weight. It was used until Britain's last hangings in 1964.
Doubt beat: Earlier "short drops" strangled people slowly; too long a drop could decapitate them.
Reversal: The most feared job had a maths table to make it fast.
Mechanism: Heavier bodies need a shorter drop for the same force; the table matched force to weight so the neck broke instantly.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_Table_of_Drops · https://www.capitalpunishmentuk.org/aberdare.html · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Marwood
Watch out: Credit for the table is disputed (Marwood vs Berry vs Aberdare Committee). Keep animation non-graphic; same tone as the firing-squad reference.

Title: Could Mirrors Really Set a Ship on Fire? 🔥
Category: other (myth test)
Era & place: 212 BC Syracuse (legend); 2005 MIT test
Cold open: "Legend says Archimedes burned Roman warships using nothing but mirrors."
True story: The "death ray" story appears centuries after Archimedes. In 2005 MIT students aimed ~127 one-foot mirrors at a stationary wooden model ship and set it alight; MythBusters failed to ignite a real boat and called it busted.
Doubt beat: MythBusters said it couldn't work in battle.
Reversal: It can work - on a still, sunny day, on a ship that doesn't move.
Mechanism: Each mirror adds its sunlight to one spot; enough mirrors push wood past its ignition point (~300°C), but a moving ship scatters the focus.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 1, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: medium (MythBusters clips + 1-2 Shorts; estimated)
Evidence: https://news.mit.edu/2005/archimedes-1005 · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_heat_ray
Watch out: Legend, not history - say "legend says". The ~300°C figure needs a source.

Title: Why People Used to Eat Mummies 🤮
Category: medical
Era & place: 12th-18th centuries, Europe
Cold open: "European doctors once prescribed ground-up Egyptian mummies for a headache."
True story: Medieval translators confused "mumia" (a bitumen used as medicine) with the black material in embalmed bodies, so ground mummy became a drug for centuries. As real mummies ran out, fakes were made from fresh corpses.
Doubt beat: Some physicians, like Guy de la Fontaine in 1564, exposed forged mummies.
Reversal: The word "mummy" itself comes from the medicine, not the other way round.
Mechanism: A translation mistake: the cure was the bitumen; Europeans thought it was the body.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: medium-high (several Shorts; estimated)
Evidence: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/mummy-eating-medical-cannibalism-gory-history · https://unisq.edu.au/news/2022/06/the-conversation-eating-mummies
Watch out: End date varies (18th vs late 19th c.). Fresh angle needed: lead with the mistranslation.

Title: Why Old Houses Have Bricked-Up Windows 🤔
Category: other (hidden reason)
Era & place: 1696-1851, England & Wales
Cold open: "In 1696, England started taxing people for every window in their house."
True story: William III's window tax charged households by number of windows; many owners blocked windows to pay less until repeal in 1851, after doctors blamed dark, unventilated homes for disease.
Doubt beat: Doctors campaigned that the tax was literally taxing light and air.
Reversal: Repealed after 155 years.
Mechanism: Windows were easy to count from the street, so the tax was easy to assess, and easy to dodge with bricks.
Fit score: 7/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 1, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: low (estimated)
Evidence: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/georgian-britain-age-modernity/window-tax/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Window_tax
Watch out: Many blank windows are decorative and predate the tax - say "some". "Daylight robbery" etymology is a myth - don't use.
