IDEA CARD
Title: The Doctor Who Drank the Cure
Category: doubted invention (medical)
Era & place: 1982-2005, Perth, Australia
Cold open: "He drank a flask of bacteria to prove every ulcer doctor wrong."
True story: Barry Marshall and Robin Warren argued that stomach ulcers were caused by the bacterium H. pylori, not stress. In 1984, with his 1984 Lancet work seen as unimportant, Marshall had a baseline endoscopy and drank a culture of H. pylori. He developed gastritis within days and cleared it with antibiotics.
Doubt beat: the consensus treated the bacterial theory as unimportant; Marshall later said nobody took it seriously (the "ridicule" framing is looser; use "skepticism / opposition").
Reversal: the self-experiment and later trials vindicated them; Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2005.
Mechanism: bacteria survive stomach acid and inflame the lining; antibiotics, not antacids, cure most ulcers.
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (4 on-topic Shorts; top 35 views; checked 2026-11-19, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Marshall ; https://mediatheque.lindau-nobel.org/laureates/marshall/cv ; https://laskerfoundation.org/?p=8839
Watch out: exact date (June vs July 1984); he got gastritis, not an ulcer; don't overstate "ridiculed".
Fresh angle: almost untouched on Shorts.

IDEA CARD
Title: The Man Who Washed His Hands in Poison
Category: backfired plan
Era & place: 1921-1924, USA
Cold open: "He poured leaded gasoline over his hands in front of reporters and breathed it in for a full minute."
True story: Thomas Midgley Jr. developed tetraethyl lead as an anti-knock gasoline additive. After workers sickened and died at production plants, he held a press conference on 30 Oct 1924, poured TEL on his hands and inhaled its vapour to show it was safe.
Doubt beat: workers hallucinating and dying at the plants (Standard Oil, New Jersey; Dayton) while the public was being reassured.
Reversal: he had already needed treatment for lead poisoning (1923) and was diagnosed again after the demo; leaded petrol was phased out worldwide decades later.
Mechanism: lead absorbs through the skin and lungs and builds up in the body; you can't see the damage at first.
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (4 on-topic Shorts; top 8 views; checked 2026-11-19, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://amp.cnn.com/cnn/2024/05/24/world/thomas-midgley-jr-leaded-gas-freon-scn ; BBC "Why did we use leaded petrol for so long?" ; https://www.toxicdocs.org/d/3Q008Xry43x1r49GLe5b120x (1924 press coverage)
Watch out: the timing of his poisoning (before vs after the demo) differs across sources; the "killed by his own pulley invention" ending is reported but check it before using.
Fresh angle: the demonstration as the cold open; the CFC sequel as an optional final twist.

IDEA CARD
Title: The Bats That Burned Down Their Own Base
Category: military oddity
Era & place: 1942-1944, USA (Carlsbad, New Mexico)
Cold open: "They strapped tiny firebombs to thousands of bats, and the bats flew straight back to base."
True story: A WWII US project (later the Navy's Project X-Ray) planned to release bats carrying small incendiaries over Japanese cities. On 15 May 1943, armed bats escaped during a test at a Carlsbad Army Airfield auxiliary base, roosted under buildings and a fuel tank, and set them on fire.
Doubt beat: an absurd-sounding idea that officials nonetheless funded and tested.
Reversal: the weapon worked, on its own airfield; the project was cancelled in 1944 as the atomic bomb took priority.
Mechanism: bats roost in dark crevices of buildings, exactly where an incendiary does the most damage.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (3 on-topic Shorts; top 58,664 views; checked 2026-11-19, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/1090bat/ ; https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Bat_bomb
Watch out: which buildings burned and the bat counts vary; get a primary or official source (Air & Space Forces Magazine is the strongest so far).
Fresh angle: tell it as a weapon that "worked perfectly", just on the wrong side.

IDEA CARD
Title: The Missile Flown by a Pigeon
Category: doubted invention
Era & place: 1940-1953, USA
Cold open: "He put three pigeons in the nose of a bomb and taught them to steer it."
True story: Psychologist B.F. Skinner trained pigeons to peck at a target's image on a screen, and the pecks steered a guided bomb. The National Defense Research Committee funded it ($25,000) as Project Pigeon; the Navy revived it as Project Orcon in 1948.
Doubt beat: Skinner: "Our problem was no one would take us seriously."
Reversal: the pigeons performed well in tests, but electronic guidance won and the project was cancelled.
Mechanism: operant conditioning; rewarded pecks become a precise tracking signal.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 1, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (1 Short with 100K+, from the B.F. Skinner Foundation; checked 2026-11-19, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Pigeon ; Skinner, "Pigeons in a Pelican" (1960) https://www.bfskinner.org/newtestsite/wp-content/uploads/2014/02/Pigeons-in-a-Pelican-pt1.pdf
Watch out: the end date (1944 vs 1953) depends on whether you mean Pigeon or Orcon; the pigeons never flew in combat.
Fresh angle: the skeptics laughed and the pigeons were better than the electronics of the time (verify the test results in Skinner's paper).

IDEA CARD
Title: Why a Newspaper Found Bat-People on the Moon
Category: hoax
Era & place: August 1835, New York
Cold open: "A newspaper announced that a famous astronomer had found winged people living on the Moon."
True story: Starting 25 Aug 1835, the New York Sun ran six articles crediting astronomer John Herschel with discovering moon unicorns, beavers and "man-bats" through a giant telescope. Herschel was in South Africa and knew nothing about it. Editor Richard Adams Locke wrote it.
Doubt beat: rival paper the New York Herald exposed it and named Locke.
Reversal: the hoax made the Sun one of the best-selling papers in the world (by its own claim, about 19,360 copies a day).
Mechanism: borrowed authority + a source too far away to deny it (no telegraph to South Africa).
Fit score: 9/10 (action 1, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (3 on-topic Shorts; top 2,096 views; checked 2026-11-19, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://daily.jstor.org/how-the-sun-conned-the-world-with-the-moon-hoax ; https://blog.library.si.edu/2013/08/great_moon_hoax_1835 ; https://hoaxes.org/archive/permalink/the_great_moon_hoax
Watch out: circulation figures are the paper's own claims; Locke later suggested it was satire.
Fresh angle: the mechanism (why nobody could check), framed as the first viral fake news.

IDEA CARD
Title: The Clock They Refused to Believe
Category: doubted invention
Era & place: 1730s-1773, England
Cold open: "A self-taught carpenter built a watch to win a national prize. The judges kept changing the rules."
True story: John Harrison spent decades building sea clocks to solve the longitude problem. His H4 watch passed sea trials, but the Board of Longitude called the result a fluke, demanded more trials and paid only part of the prize, with conditions.
Doubt beat: the Board (dominated by astronomers favouring the lunar method) repeatedly withheld the money.
Reversal: King George III tested his H5 personally and backed him; Parliament paid him £8,750 in 1773, though he never officially won the prize.
Mechanism: if you know home-port time precisely, the time difference with local noon gives your longitude.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 1, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (top 145,711 views, mostly horology clips; checked 2026-11-19, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/john-harrison ; https://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/john-harrison ; https://www.Wikipedia.com/wiki/Longitude_prize
Watch out: H4's exact accuracy numbers vary by voyage; the location of the king's test differs (Richmond / Kew).
Fresh angle: underdog vs institution; the king as the deus ex machina.

Title: The Day Sweden Switched Sides
Category: absurd institution
Era & place: 3 September 1967, Sweden
Cold open: "At 4:50 in the morning, every car in Sweden stopped, crossed the road and waited."
True story: On Dagen H, Sweden switched from driving on the left to the right in one morning. Traffic halted, cars moved to the other side, and the switch went live at 5:00 a.m. The Riksdag approved it in 1963.
Doubt beat: widespread public opposition (a 1955 referendum rejected the change by a large majority; verify).
Reversal: accidents fell after the switch (only 125 reported the following Monday vs a normal 130-198).
Mechanism: people afraid of the change drove more carefully (plus temporary lower speed limits); risk compensation.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 1)
Competition: low (4 on-topic Shorts; top 2 views; checked 2026-11-19, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagen_H ; https://99percentinvisible.org/episode/h-day/transcript/ ; https://www.mentalfloss.com/history/day-everyone-sweden-had-start-driving-other-side-road
Watch out: confirm the 1955 referendum numbers; the long-term accident drop is partly due to other measures.
Fresh angle: a country breaking its own habit overnight, and getting safer.

Title: The Ghost That Knocked on Command
Category: hoax
Era & place: 1762, Smithfield, London
Cold open: "Half of London crowded into a tiny bedroom to ask a ghost questions. It knocked once for yes."
True story: The "Cock Lane ghost" ("Scratching Fanny") supposedly accused a man of murder through knocks. Crowds blocked the street; Samuel Johnson joined an investigation. The knocks were traced to 12-year-old Elizabeth Parsons, who was seen hiding a small wooden board.
Doubt beat: skeptics, including Johnson, investigated while London's elite queued to attend.
Reversal: her father Richard Parsons was convicted of conspiracy, pilloried and imprisoned; the crowd at the pillory reportedly collected money for him.
Mechanism: a hidden board + leading questions + crowd expectation.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 1)
Competition: unknown (search returned no data; re-check)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cock_Lane_ghost ; https://www.drjohnsonshouse.org/post/the-cock-lane-ghost ; Paul Chambers, *The Cock Lane Ghost* (2006)
Watch out: details of the pillory (location and number of sessions) vary; the original accusation's truth is unknown.
Fresh angle: the "murder accusation by ghost" twist plus a famous investigator.

IDEA CARD
Title: Why Australia's Toads Never Ate the Beetles
Category: backfired plan
Era & place: 1935, Queensland, Australia
Cold open: "They shipped 102 toads across the Pacific to save Australia's sugar crop."
True story: The Bureau of Sugar Experimental Stations imported 102 cane toads from Hawaii to Meringa near Cairns, bred thousands and released them to eat cane beetles. The toads spread across northern Australia and became an invasive pest.
Doubt beat: weak in the sources found so far (look for warnings at the time, e.g. entomologist Walter Froggatt; verify).
Reversal: the beetles lived high on the cane stalks where the toads couldn't reach, and the toads ate everything else instead.
Mechanism: a predator that can't reach its prey; no natural enemies + toxic skin = population explosion.
Fit score: 7/10 (action 1, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: low (top Shorts are wildlife clips, not the history; checked 2026-11-19, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://digital-classroom.nma.gov.au/defining-moments/cane-toads-introduced (National Museum of Australia) ; https://news.mongabay.com/2005/04/cane-toads-increasingly-a-problem-in-australia/
Watch out: release numbers vary (2,400 vs 3,000); "couldn't jump high enough" is common but verify with a scholarly source.
Fresh angle: the single physical reason the plan failed.

IDEA CARD
Title: The Man Who Said the Continents Move
Category: doubted idea (science)
Era & place: 1912-1960s, Germany / Greenland
Cold open: "He cut out the continents on a map and showed they fit together like a puzzle."
True story: Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift (1912/1915). At a 1926 meeting of the American Association of Petroleum Geologists, experts attacked every line of his evidence. He died on the Greenland ice sheet in 1930.
Doubt beat: rejected by most geologists in his lifetime.
Reversal: seafloor and palaeomagnetism discoveries in the 1950s-60s made plate tectonics accepted geology.
Mechanism: he had the evidence (matching coastlines, fossils, rocks) but not the engine, which turned out to be the seafloor spreading.
Fit score: 7/10 (action 1, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 2) - the reversal comes after his death, so it's less punchy
Competition: medium (top results are general continental-drift explainers with up to 3.7M views; few tell his story)
Evidence: https://www.britannica.com/biography/Alfred-Wegener ; https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/history/wegener.html ; https://www.discovermagazine.com/continental-drift-a-revolutionary-theory-that-was-once-considered-42258
Watch out: avoid claiming he was "laughed at" generally; the 1926 meeting is the documented doubt.
Fresh angle: a single scene (the 1926 meeting) as the story spine.
