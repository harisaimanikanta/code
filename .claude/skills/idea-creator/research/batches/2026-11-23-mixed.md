Title: The Test That Said the Warship Would Sink
Category: engineering
Era & place: 1628, Stockholm
Cold open: "He ordered thirty sailors to run across the deck of the king's new warship." (14 words)
True story: Before Vasa's maiden voyage, a stability test was run at the quay: about 30 men ran side to side and the ship rolled so hard that Admiral Klas Fleming stopped it after three runs. She sailed anyway on 10 August 1628 and capsized in Stockholm harbour within minutes, about 1,300 m out.
Doubt beat: The test itself was the warning; the admiral stopped it, but the voyage went ahead because the king was waiting for his ship.
Reversal: The most powerful warship in the Baltic sank in the first breeze, in front of crowds.
Mechanism: Too much weight high up (a second gun deck of heavy cannon, insisted on by the king) and too little ballast low down: the centre of gravity was above the point where the hull could right itself.
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: medium (5+ on-topic Shorts in a web search, views unknown; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://www.vasamuseet.se/en/vasa-history/inquest · https://www.britannica.com/topic/Vasa-warship
Watch out: Timing of the test (days vs weeks before) varies; inquiry outcome disputed (no one punished vs blame on the dead shipwright Hybertsson). Don't claim the king personally ordered "sail anyway".
Fresh angle: Competitors say "it sank in 20 minutes"; open on the stability test that predicted it.

Title: Why Europe Called the Wright Brothers Liars
Category: doubted invention
Era & place: 1906-1908, Paris / Le Mans
Cold open: "He pulled his flying machine out of a shed at a French horse-racing track." (14 words)
True story: After 1903 the Wrights flew in near-secret in Ohio, refusing to demonstrate without a contract. In 1906 the Paris edition of the New York Herald headlined "Fliers or Liars?" and French papers called them "bluffeurs". On 8 August 1908 Wilbur flew publicly at Hunaudières near Le Mans, banking and circling with a control nobody in Europe had.
Doubt beat: "Fliers or Liars?" (Paris Herald, 10 Feb 1906); French aviators and press dismissed their claims.
Reversal: One flight of under two minutes at Le Mans turned the "bluffers" into heroes; French aviators conceded they had been beaten.
Mechanism: Wing-warping gave roll control - they could bank into a turn like a bird - while European machines mostly steered flat with a rudder, like boats.
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: medium (many Wright Brothers Shorts, none on the "liars" angle found; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/you-just-invented-airplane-now-what · https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/sets/detail/the-wrights-wowing-the-world · https://www.smithsonianmag.com/air-space-magazine/i-have-today-seen-wilbur-wright-and-his-great-white-bird-3899606/
Watch out: Exact French quotes after the flight (e.g. Blériot's, "Nous sommes battus") need primary-source checking. Don't say "nobody believed them" - some did.
Fresh angle: Everyone covers Kitty Hawk 1903; the real WTF is five years of being called frauds.

Title: The Vacuum Cleaner That Came With Free Flights
Category: backfired plan
Era & place: 1992-1993, UK
Cold open: "He bought a vacuum cleaner he didn't need to get two plane tickets." (13 words)
True story: In August 1992 Hoover's UK arm offered two free return flights (Europe, later the US) to anyone spending £100+ on a Hoover product. Hoover expected around 50,000 claims; it got roughly 220,000-300,000+ and couldn't meet them. Customers sued, BBC Watchdog investigated, and Hoover Europe was later sold to Candy.
Doubt beat: Hoover assumed buyers would be deterred by conditions and paperwork; customers and Watchdog called it a scam when flights didn't materialise.
Reversal: A promotion meant to clear stock cost far more than the sales it generated (estimates commonly ~£48m+); executives were sacked.
Mechanism: Ticket value exceeded product price - a free flight worth more than the vacuum - so rational buyers treated vacuums as a coupon for flights.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (no on-topic Shorts found; long-form docs exist; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: http://news.bbc.co.uk (BBC "Hoover's free flights fiasco recalled", 2004 - locate URL) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_free_flights_promotion · https://www.marketingweek.com/hoover-fails-to-shake-off-free-flights-horror/
Watch out: Loss figures vary widely (£48m-£100m+); claim count varies. Royal Warrant claim unverified - don't use.
Fresh angle: Frame as a maths problem: the prize cost more than the product.

Title: The Critics Who Praised a Chimpanzee
Category: hoax
Era & place: 1964, Gothenburg, Sweden
Cold open: "He handed a paintbrush to a four-year-old chimpanzee at the zoo." (12 words)
True story: Journalist Åke "Dacke" Axelsson of Göteborgs-Tidningen had Peter, a chimp at Borås zoo, make paintings, then exhibited four as the work of French avant-garde artist "Pierre Brassau". Most critics praised them.
Doubt beat: One lone critic wrote "Only an ape could have done this" - the only one who was right.
Reversal: Axelsson revealed the painter was a chimpanzee; the critic Rolf Anderberg reportedly held that it was still the best painting in the show.
Mechanism: Critics judge with context: a French name and a gallery wall made people read brushstrokes as intention.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 2)
Competition: low (1-2 on-topic Shorts, one in Polish; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,870835,00.html (Time 1964) · https://hoaxes.org/archive/permalink/pierre_brassau_monkey_artist
Watch out: Paper names (Göteborgs-Tidningen vs Göteborgs-Posten) vary; the critic quotes need the original reviews. A "Banghi" variant is unverified.
Fresh angle: Twist ending - the one critic who panned it, and the critic who stood by his praise.

Title: The Spy Whose Agents Didn't Exist
Category: espionage
Era & place: 1941-1944, Lisbon / London
Cold open: "He wrote reports about British ports he had never seen, using a tourist guidebook." (14 words)
True story: Juan Pujol García offered to spy for Britain and was turned away, so he became a German agent and invented reports from "London" while living in Lisbon. MI5 then recruited him as "Garbo"; he built a network of ~27 fictional sub-agents and sent 500+ messages before D-Day, then convinced Berlin that Normandy was a diversion.
Doubt beat: The British rejected him repeatedly before he proved himself by fooling the Germans on his own.
Reversal: Germany awarded him the Iron Cross; Britain later gave him an MBE - honoured by both sides.
Mechanism: The Germans checked messages against each other - but all sources were Garbo, so they "confirmed" each other.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: medium (several Garbo Shorts incl. "How 1 Man Tricked Hitler"; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://www.mi5.gov.uk/agent-garbo · https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/d-day-spies
Watch out: Iron Cross class (1st vs 2nd) varies; "only person with both" is disputed; sub-agent count varies (27 is common).
Fresh angle: Start before MI5 - he faked spying from Lisbon with a guidebook and railway timetables, uninvited.

Title: The Poet Who Was Written in One Afternoon
Category: hoax
Era & place: 1943-1944, Australia
Cold open: "He pulled random lines from a dictionary and a mosquito-control report to write poetry." (15 words)
True story: Poets James McAuley and Harold Stewart invented "Ern Malley", a dead young modernist, writing his whole oeuvre in one afternoon from random sources, and sent it to Max Harris's Angry Penguins, which published it in 1944.
Doubt beat: The hoaxers mocked modernism as meaningless; when revealed, the press ridiculed Harris.
Reversal: Harris was then prosecuted and fined for publishing "indecent" poems - by an author who never existed. Decades later, Ern Malley became celebrated as real poetry.
Mechanism: Modernist verse relied on free association, so random fragments were indistinguishable from intention to a reader expecting genius.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 2)
Competition: low (no on-topic Shorts found; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://jacket2.org/jacket1/court-transcript-trial-max-harris (trial transcript) · https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/node/204921 · https://www.adelaide.edu.au/lumen/news/list/2024/09/09/ern-malley-and-the-angry-penguins
Watch out: The "mosquito report" and specific sources must be checked in the hoaxers' own statement; fine amount (£5) to confirm.
Fresh angle: The double twist - a fake poet got a real man convicted, then became genuinely famous.

Title: The Night Telegraphs Ran Without Batteries
Category: other (science)
Era & place: 2 September 1859, Boston-Portland (Maine)
Cold open: "He unplugged the batteries from his telegraph, and kept sending messages." (11 words)
True story: During the Carrington solar storm, telegraph lines across Europe and North America failed, sparked and shocked operators. On the Boston-Portland line, operators disconnected their batteries and worked the line for about two hours on current induced by the aurora alone, as reported in the Boston Evening Traveler.
Doubt beat: The operators themselves didn't trust it - Boston asked Portland to cut the battery "for fifteen minutes" as a test. (Weak; no public mockery.)
Reversal: The storm that wrecked the network powered it.
Mechanism: The geomagnetic storm changed Earth's magnetic field, inducing current in long wires grounded at each end - a natural battery.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low-medium (several Carrington Shorts, one on telegraphs; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrington_Event (cites Loomis 1860, American Journal of Science, reprinting the operators' exchange) · https://research.noaa.gov/?p=22740
Watch out: Wording of the transcript varies between reprints; use Loomis (1860) as primary. Don't claim worldwide fires as fact beyond documented reports.
Fresh angle: Most Carrington Shorts are doom ("could it happen again?"); this is the bizarre upside.

Title: The Physics Paper That Was Pure Nonsense
Category: hoax
Era & place: 1996, New York
Cold open: "He wrote that physical reality is a social construct, and a journal published it." (14 words)
True story: NYU physicist Alan Sokal submitted "Transgressing the Boundaries: Towards a Transformative Hermeneutics of Quantum Gravity" to Social Text, which published it in its 1996 "Science Wars" issue. Weeks later he revealed in Lingua Franca that it was a deliberate parody.
Doubt beat: Sokal doubted the field's standards; the editors accused him of unethical deception.
Reversal: The journal that was critiquing science was fooled by a scientist; the story made the front page of the New York Times.
Mechanism: No outside physicist reviewed it; it flattered the editors' views and cited their heroes, so the jargon passed.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 1, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (no on-topic Shorts found; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://physics.nyu.edu/faculty/sokal/ (Sokal's own archive) · https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1996/08/08/sokals-hoax/ · https://www.chronicle.com/article/article-on-hermeneutics-of-quantum-gravity-revealed-as-hoax/
Watch out: Politically charged; keep it about the editing failure. The NYT front-page claim needs checking.
Fresh angle: Visual hook: quote the nonsense lines on screen.

Title: The Fake Officer Who Moved the Stock Market
Category: con
Era & place: 21 February 1814, Dover to London
Cold open: "He rode into Dover in a red officer's uniform, shouting that Napoleon was dead." (14 words)
True story: A man posing as "Colonel du Bourg" brought false news of Napoleon's death; government stock jumped on the London exchange. The plotters had bought stock in advance and sold into the rise. Charles de Berenger, Andrew Cochrane-Johnstone and naval hero Lord Thomas Cochrane were convicted.
Doubt beat: Messengers to Whitehall came back with no confirmation; prices slumped and the Stock Exchange committee investigated.
Reversal: The trick worked for hours, then the trail led to a national hero, who was jailed and expelled from Parliament - and re-elected.
Mechanism: News moved at horse speed; fog reportedly stopped the Admiralty shutter telegraph from confirming or denying it, so traders had nothing to check.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: low (no on-topic Shorts found; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://gutenberg.org/ebooks/21027 (trial transcript) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Stock_Exchange_Fraud_of_1814 · https://moneyweek.com/investments/investment-strategy/600903/great-frauds-in-history-andrew-cochrane-johnstone
Watch out: Cochrane's guilt is disputed by historians (Lloyd 1947, Cordingly 2007 say innocent) - don't call him guilty. Fog/semaphore detail is single-sourced; check Richard Dale's "Napoleon is Dead" (2006).
Fresh angle: An 1814 fake-news pump-and-dump.

Title: The Crown Thief the King Rewarded
Category: con
Era & place: 9 May 1671, Tower of London
Cold open: "He flattened the king's crown with a mallet and hid it under his cloak." (14 words)
True story: Colonel Thomas Blood befriended the jewel keeper while disguised as a parson, then returned with accomplices, attacked the keeper, flattened the crown and fled with the regalia. He was caught at the gates and refused to speak to anyone but Charles II.
Doubt beat: The court expected his execution; the Duke of Ormonde (whom Blood had earlier tried to kidnap) was baffled by what followed.
Reversal: Charles II pardoned him and granted him Irish lands worth £500 a year; he became a figure at court.
Mechanism: Why the king pardoned him is unknown - theories range from charm to Blood being useful as an informer. Mechanism is the weak spot.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 2)
Competition: low-medium (2+ crown-jewel heist Shorts; checked 2026-11-23, estimated)
Evidence: https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/the-king-the-crown-the-colonel/ · https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1953/colonel-blood--the-theft-of-the-crown-jewels/ · https://www.history.com/articles/the-man-who-stole-the-crown-jewels
Watch out: Reasons for the pardon are speculation - present as a mystery. Pension amount unknown.
Fresh angle: Payoff is the reward, not the heist.
