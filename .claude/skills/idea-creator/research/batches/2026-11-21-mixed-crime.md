Title: The Killer Who Asked the Police if He Could Be Traced
Category: crime
Era & place: 2004-2005, Wichita, Kansas
Cold open: "He wrote to the police and asked: if I send you a floppy disk, can you trace it?"
True story: After decades of silence, the BTK killer resumed taunting police in 2004. He asked in writing whether a floppy disk could be traced and told them to answer with a newspaper ad. Police placed the ad ("Rex, it will be OK"). He mailed a disk to a TV station in February 2005; its metadata showed "Christ Lutheran Church" and the user "Dennis". Dennis Rader, the church council president, was arrested on 25 February 2005 after a DNA match.
Doubt beat: he trusted the police's answer; they had told him what he wanted to hear.
Reversal: the disk he believed was safe identified him in days.
Mechanism: deleted/hidden file metadata (last saved by "Dennis" at "Christ Lutheran Church") plus a familial DNA match.
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (1 on-topic Short; top 1 view; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://www.cbsnews.com/news/computer-trail-led-to-btk-suspect ; https://www.abajournal.com/magazine/article/how_the_cops_caught_btk ; https://www2.ljworld.com/news/2005/mar/04/computer_disk_dna/
Watch out: victims and families exist; no glorification, no crime-scene detail. The TV station name varies (KSAS vs KAKE). The DNA came from his daughter's medical sample (verify wording).
Fresh angle: the newspaper-ad lie as the turning point.

Title: The Man Who Didn't Trust the Nuclear Alarm
Category: other (Cold War / doubted machine)
Era & place: 26 September 1983, Serpukhov-15 bunker near Moscow
Cold open: "The siren screamed, the screen said LAUNCH, and he decided the computer was lying."
True story: Lt. Col. Stanislav Petrov was duty officer when the new Oko early-warning system reported one, then up to five US missile launches, three weeks after the Soviets shot down KAL 007. He judged it a false alarm and didn't treat it as a real attack. It was: sunlight reflecting off high clouds had fooled the satellites.
Doubt beat: the machine said attack; he doubted it. A real strike with five missiles made no sense to him, and ground radar saw nothing.
Reversal: he was right; the story stayed secret until the 1990s.
Mechanism: rare alignment of sunlight on high-altitude clouds with the satellites' orbits.
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (7 on-topic Shorts; top 12,927; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://www.britannica.com/biography/Stanislav-Petrov ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Soviet_nuclear_false_alarm_incident ; https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2026/09/1983-averting-potential-nuclear-war
Watch out: "saved the world" is debated (others were in the chain of command); accounts differ on what he reported upward; reprimand stories are not confirmed.
Fresh angle: the sunlight-on-clouds mechanism.

Title: The Doctor Caught by His Own Typewriter
Category: crime
Era & place: 1998, Hyde, Greater Manchester
Cold open: "He typed a fake will on his own office typewriter, leaving a fortune to himself."
True story: Dr Harold Shipman forged the will of his patient Kathleen Grundy, a former mayoress, leaving him £386,000. Her daughter, a solicitor, found it suspicious. The will was traced to a Brother typewriter he owned, her body was exhumed, and she had died of a morphine overdose given within hours of his visit. Shipman was convicted of 15 murders; an inquiry linked him to about 250 deaths.
Doubt beat: his patients trusted him completely for decades; nobody questioned a GP.
Reversal: a clumsy forgery ended one of history's worst serial-killing careers.
Mechanism: typewriter forensics, a forged signature, and an exhumation that found morphine.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (2 on-topic Shorts; top 10 views; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Shipman ; https://www.crimeandinvestigation.co.uk/crime-files/harold-shipman ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC318504
Watch out: the "~250 victims" is the Shipman Inquiry's estimate, convictions were 15; the will amount (£386,000) should be checked; whether he wanted to be caught is speculation.
Fresh angle: almost no Shorts.

Title: The Man Who Got a Real Bank to Print Fake Money
Category: con
Era & place: 1924-1925, Lisbon and London
Cold open: "He forged one contract, and the Bank of Portugal's real printer made him real money."
True story: Artur Alves Reis forged a contract supposedly from the Bank of Portugal and used it to get Waterlow & Sons, the bank's genuine printer in London, to print about 200,000 real 500-escudo notes. Because they were printed on the real plates, they were indistinguishable from legal notes. He founded his own bank with the money.
Doubt beat: nobody doubted notes made by the real printer.
Reversal: exposed in December 1925 when duplicate serial numbers were found; a newspaper broke the story; Waterlow was ordered to pay the bank about £610,000.
Mechanism: the notes weren't fake; only the paperwork was. Duplicate serial numbers gave him away.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (2 on-topic Shorts; top 0 views; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alves_dos_Reis ; https://www.lse.ac.uk/asset-library/information/wp8204.pdf ; https://www.portugalresident.com/alves-reis-and-the-banknote-crisis-of-1925/
Watch out: the claimed link to the 1926 coup is contested; note counts vary.
Fresh angle: "the money was real" is the twist.

Title: The Cook Who Didn't Believe She Was Sick
Category: medical
Era & place: 1906-1938, New York
Cold open: "A man followed a cook from house to house, because everywhere she worked, people got typhoid."
True story: Sanitary engineer George Soper traced a 1906 outbreak to cook Mary Mallon, a healthy carrier of typhoid who had no symptoms. She refused to give samples and was quarantined on North Brother Island in 1907. Released on condition she stop cooking, she was found in 1915 cooking at Sloane Maternity Hospital under the name "Mary Brown", after an outbreak there (25 sick, 2 dead). She spent the rest of her life on the island.
Doubt beat: she didn't believe it; she had never been ill.
Reversal: the healthy woman was the source; she was isolated for about 26 years.
Mechanism: asymptomatic carriers: bacteria live in the gallbladder and spread through food.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: low (10 on-topic Shorts; 2 with 100K+ views, top 152,643; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/typhoid-mary-tragic-tale-exposed-health-impacts-super-spreaders ; https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/typhoid-marys-life-sentence-quarantine ; https://origins.osu.edu/read/who-was-typhoid-mary
Watch out: death counts attributed to her vary widely; historians stress she was treated harshly (other carriers weren't jailed). Avoid "villain" framing.
Fresh angle: she was the only carrier locked up for life.

Title: The Glowing Paint They Told Women to Lick
Category: medical
Era & place: 1917-1928, Orange, New Jersey (and Ottawa, Illinois)
Cold open: "She dipped her brush in glowing paint, and put it between her lips to make a sharp point."
True story: Women painting watch dials with radium paint at the US Radium Corporation were taught to point their brushes with their lips. They began to suffer jaw decay, anaemia and cancers. Grace Fryer and four others sued in 1927; the case settled in 1928.
Doubt beat: the company insisted the paint was safe and blamed other causes.
Reversal: the cases helped establish worker-safety rules and the right to sue for occupational disease.
Mechanism: the body treats radium like calcium and stores it in the bones.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: low (9 on-topic Shorts; 3 with 100K+ views, top 3,679,009; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search). One very big Short exists.
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_Girls ; https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/radium-girls/ ; https://www.historynet.com/radium-girls-vs-us-radium/
Watch out: "lip, dip, paint" is a well-known phrase; check its origin. Settlement details vary.
Fresh angle: the calcium mechanism.

Title: The Brother Who Recognised a Sentence
Category: crime
Era & place: 1995-1996, USA
Cold open: "He read a terrorist's manifesto in the newspaper, and recognised his own brother's voice."
True story: In 1995 the Washington Post and New York Times published the Unabomber's manifesto at the FBI's request. David Kaczynski's wife Linda Patrik urged him to read it; he recognised his brother Ted's ideas and phrasing and compared it with old letters. He went to the FBI; Ted was arrested in April 1996.
Doubt beat: David didn't want to believe it, and agonised before going to the FBI.
Reversal: after 17 years, the case broke because a brother recognised the writing.
Mechanism: linguistic fingerprints: distinctive phrases (e.g. "you can't eat your cake and have it too") matched in letters.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 1)
Competition: low (1 on-topic Short; top 9 views; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search)

Title: The Fake Town That Became Real
Category: other (maps)
Era & place: 1930s-1990s, Catskills, New York
Cold open: "Two mapmakers drew a town that didn't exist, just to catch anyone who copied them."
True story: Otto Lindberg and Ernest Alpers of General Drafting put a fake town, "Agloe", on a dirt-road crossing on their Esso road map as a copyright trap. When Agloe appeared on a Rand McNally map, they cried theft, but Rand McNally said Agloe was real: there was now an "Agloe General Store" there, named after the map.
Doubt beat: the mapmakers were sure they had caught a copier.
Reversal: the trap made the fake town real.
Mechanism: "paper towns" / trap streets as fingerprints for copied maps.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 1, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: low (6 on-topic Shorts; no views; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search)

Title: The Killer Found Through a Cousin's DNA
Category: crime
Era & place: 1974-1986 crimes; arrest April 2018, California
Cold open: "Investigators uploaded a killer's DNA to a family-tree website, and found his cousins."
True story: The Golden State Killer committed at least 13 murders and dozens of rapes in California and was never identified. In 2018 investigators uploaded crime-scene DNA to the genealogy site GEDmatch, found distant relatives, built family trees and narrowed it to Joseph DeAngelo, a former police officer. A discarded DNA sample matched. He pleaded guilty in 2020.
Doubt beat: the case was cold for decades.
Reversal: the killer was a former cop, caught through relatives' hobby DNA.
Mechanism: investigative genetic genealogy: shared DNA with third cousins + family trees.

Title: The Funhouse Dummy That Was a Real Corpse
Category: other
Era & place: 1911-1977, Oklahoma to Long Beach, California
Cold open: "A TV crew moved a hanging dummy in a funhouse, and its arm snapped off. Inside was bone."
True story: In December 1976, while filming The Six Million Dollar Man at the Nu-Pike amusement park in Long Beach, a crew member moved a glow-painted "hanging man" prop; an arm came off, revealing human bone. It was Elmer McCurdy, an outlaw killed in 1911, whose embalmed body had been exhibited at carnivals for 65 years. He was buried in Guthrie, Oklahoma, in 1977.
Doubt beat: for decades everyone assumed the body was a wax dummy.
Reversal: the prop was a real man.
Mechanism: heavy arsenic embalming preserved him; unclaimed, he was sold and resold as an attraction.
Fit score: 7/10 (action 2, doubt 0, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 2)
Competition: low (14 on-topic Shorts; top 19,032; checked 2026-11-21, yt-dlp search)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_McCurdy ; https://www.history.com/articles/elmer-mccurdy-mummy-dead-outlaw ; https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/2018/07/elmer-mccurdy-traveling-corpse/
Watch out: the discovery year is given as 1976 or 1977; funhouse names vary.
Fresh angle: the 65-year journey told backwards.
