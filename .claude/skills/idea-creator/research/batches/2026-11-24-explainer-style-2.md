Title: Why Old Graves Were Locked in Iron Cages 😨
Category: other (hidden reason)
Era & place: c.1816-1832, Scotland (Greyfriars, Edinburgh; Aberdeenshire)
Cold open: "Families locked their dead relatives inside heavy iron cages."
True story: Anatomy schools needed bodies and the legal supply (executed criminals) was tiny, so "resurrection men" dug up fresh graves and sold the corpses. Families bought mortsafes - iron cages or heavy slabs over the grave - plus watchtowers and grave guards.
Doubt beat: Cemetery walls and night watchmen weren't enough; robbers dug fast and at night.
Reversal: The Anatomy Act 1832 gave schools a legal supply of unclaimed bodies, and the cages became useless almost overnight.
Mechanism: A body is only worth stealing for a few weeks before it decays; the cage just had to stay locked until then, and was then moved to the next grave.
Fit score: 10/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low-medium (1-2 on-topic Shorts; checked 2026-11-24, estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortsafe · https://www.abdn.ac.uk/bodysnatchers/background.php · https://wellcomecollection.org/articles/WxfvsiQAACcAY5Kp
Watch out: "Invented 1816" is one source; say "early 1800s". Some mortsafes were reused/rented - confirm before claiming.
Fresh angle: Tie to Burke and Hare (who murdered instead of digging) as the second reveal.

Title: Why Switching to Limes Brought Scurvy Back 🍋
Category: backfired plan
Era & place: 1795-1870s, Royal Navy
Cold open: "The Navy swapped one fruit for another, and sailors started dying again."
True story: From 1795 the Royal Navy issued lemon juice and scurvy largely vanished. In the mid-1800s it switched to West Indian limes from British colonies; limes have far less vitamin C, and the juice was often degraded by air and copper pipes. Scurvy returned, e.g. on the 1875-76 Nares Arctic expedition.
Doubt beat: Officials assumed "acid" was the cure, so any sour citrus would do.
Reversal: The cure was lost - and British sailors were nicknamed "limeys" after the worse fruit.
Mechanism: Vitamin C, not sourness, prevents scurvy; limes have roughly half to a third as much, and processing destroyed more.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 1, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (no on-topic Shorts found; estimated)
Evidence: https://usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2021/february/finding-cure-scurvy · https://bluesci.co.uk/posts/forgotten-knowledge · https://askabiologist.asu.edu/explore/sailors-called-limeys
Watch out: Switch date (~1860s) needs a naval-history source; the Nares link is partly about long-stored juice. Don't overstate deaths.

Title: Why Old Maps Almost Never Said "Here Be Dragons" 🐉
Category: hoax / myth bust
Era & place: c.1504-1510 (Hunt-Lenox Globe)
Cold open: "Everyone 'knows' old maps warned 'Here be dragons.' Almost none did."
True story: The Latin "HC SVNT DRACONES" is known on only the Hunt-Lenox Globe (and the related Ostrich Egg Globe), near the coast of East Asia. Mapmakers more often drew sea monsters or wrote other notes for unknown lands.
Doubt beat: It's repeated in films, games and books as a standard map warning.
Reversal: A phrase on one small globe became "what all old maps said".
Mechanism: Popular culture repeated a vivid line until it felt like history (scholars still debate what that globe's note even meant).
Fit score: 9/10 (action 1, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low (no myth-bust Shorts found; estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Here_be_dragons · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunt%E2%80%93Lenox_Globe · https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/hunt-lenox-globe-here-be-dragons
Watch out: Globe date varies (c.1504-1510). "Hic sunt leones" being common is also debated - don't claim it as fact.

Title: How Engineers Saved the Leaning Tower by Digging 🤔
Category: engineering
Era & place: 1990-2001, Pisa
Cold open: "To stop the tower falling, engineers dug soil out from under it."
True story: By 1990 the lean was near collapse and the tower closed. After lead weights and cables, engineers drilled under the north (high) side and removed small amounts of soil (~38 m³ in total); the tower settled back, straightening by about 38-50 cm. It reopened in December 2001.
Doubt beat: Earlier fixes (an 1838 trench, 1990s attempts) made the lean worse.
Reversal: Taking ground away - not adding support - saved it.
Mechanism: Removing soil under the high side lets that side sink slightly, rotating the tower back toward vertical.
Fit score: 9/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 2, evidence 1)
Competition: medium (3-4 Shorts; estimated)
Evidence: https://ingenia.org.uk/articles/the-stabilisation-of-the-leaning-tower-of-pisa/ · https://www.ice.org.uk/what-is-civil-engineering/infrastructure-projects/stabilising-the-leaning-tower-of-pisa
Watch out: Straightening figure 38 cm (ICE) vs ~50 cm elsewhere; dates 1999-2001.

Title: Why Venice Is Standing on a Forest Underwater 🌲
Category: engineering
Era & place: from c.9th century, Venice
Cold open: "Under Venice there are millions of tree trunks, and they haven't rotted."
True story: Venetian buildings stand on wooden piles (mostly alder, also larch, oak, pine, elm) driven into the lagoon mud. Many have lasted centuries.
Doubt beat: Wood in water should rot.
Reversal: Underwater, it lasts far longer than on land.
Mechanism: Buried in waterlogged, oxygen-poor mud, the fungi that rot wood can barely survive; decay slows to a crawl.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 1, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: low-medium (1 Short + long videos; estimated)
Evidence: https://www.britannica.com/technology/How-Was-Venice-Built-on-Water · https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:baf4c9bc-bda9-4f1d-878b-ae8b9cd020eb
Watch out: "Millions of piles" and "never rot" are popular exaggerations; bacteria still degrade them slowly. Say "slowed".

Title: Why Napoleon Always Had His Hand in His Coat 🤔
Category: myth bust
Era & place: 1700s-1812
Cold open: "Napoleon's famous hand-in-coat pose wasn't his idea at all."
True story: The hand-in-waistcoat pose was a common 18th-century portrait convention signalling calm, gentlemanly bearing (described in Nivelon's etiquette book, 1737/38). Napoleon got linked to it mainly through David's 1812 portrait, which he didn't even sit for.
Doubt beat: Popular myths say stomach ache, a skin disease or hiding a hand.
Reversal: It was a painters' cliché decades before he was born.
Mechanism: Portrait painters repeated a pose that meant "manly boldness tempered with modesty".
Fit score: 8/10 (action 1, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 2)
Competition: medium (3 Shorts; estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand-in-waistcoat · https://www.nga.gov/collection/highlights/david-the-emperor-napoleon-in-his-study-at-the-tuileries.html · https://www.napoleon-series.org/faq/c_hand.html
Watch out: Nivelon date 1737 vs 1738.

Title: Why Big Ben Runs on Old Pennies 🪙
Category: other (hidden reason)
Era & place: Westminster, London (ongoing)
Cold open: "To fix the world's most famous clock, someone climbs up and adds a penny."
True story: Big Ben's clock is regulated by stacking pre-decimal pennies on the pendulum; each penny makes it gain about 0.4 seconds a day (Parliament spokesperson).
Doubt beat: An 11-ton clock adjusted with pocket change sounds like a joke.
Reversal: It's the official method.
Mechanism: A penny shifts the pendulum's effective centre of mass up, shortening it, so it swings slightly faster.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 1, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: medium-high (4+ Shorts; estimated)
Evidence: https://www.britannica.com/topic/Big-Ben-clock-London · https://www.cbsnews.com/news/british-admit-big-bens-bongs-not-quite-on-time
Watch out: Some sources say 0.2 s; use "about two-fifths of a second" (Parliament).
Fresh angle: The 2015 drift of six seconds as the hook.

Title: Why Barber Poles Are Red and White 💈
Category: medical
Era & place: medieval Europe; Company of Barber-Surgeons 1540
Cold open: "Your barber's pole is a sign for bloodletting."
True story: Barber-surgeons cut hair but also pulled teeth and let blood. Patients gripped a staff to make veins stand out, and bandages were hung to dry outside as an advert - the origin of the striped pole.
Doubt beat: Medicine and haircuts in the same shop sounds absurd.
Reversal: The pole is a leftover medical sign still on every street.
Mechanism: Red = blood, white = bandages; the spiral = bandages wrapped around the staff.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 1, mechanism 2, evidence 2)
Competition: medium (3 Shorts; estimated)
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barber%27s_pole · https://www.history.com/articles/why-are-barber-poles-red-white-and-blue
Watch out: The bandage story is traditional, not proven; the 1540 colour rules are disputed. Blue is probably American patriotism.

Title: Did Coffin Bells Ever Save Anyone? 🔔
Category: myth test
Era & place: 18th-19th century, Europe and US
Cold open: "Some coffins came with a string tied to a bell above the grave."
True story: Fear of premature burial produced dozens of patented safety coffins (30+ designs in Germany alone), many with bells. But there's no documented case of anyone saved by one.
Doubt beat: Countless Shorts say "saved by the bell" comes from this.
Reversal: The phrase comes from boxing (the bell ending a round), and the coffins mostly went unused.
Mechanism: Bloating bodies could tug the string, so bells would have rung for the dead anyway.
Fit score: 8/10 (action 2, doubt 2, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 1)
Competition: high (8+ Shorts) - fresh angle: the debunk; most competitors repeat the myth.
Evidence: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safety_coffin · https://australian.museum/about/history/exhibitions/death-the-last-taboo/safety-coffins/ · https://www.ripleys.com/stories/being-buried-alive
Watch out: "No one was ever saved" is an absence claim - say "no documented case".

Title: Why Jeans Have Tiny Metal Rivets 👖
Category: doubted invention
Era & place: 1871-1873, Reno and San Francisco
Cold open: "A tailor fixed ripping work pants with the rivets he used on horse blankets."
True story: Reno tailor Jacob Davis riveted the pocket corners of work pants that kept tearing. He couldn't afford the patent, so he partnered with cloth supplier Levi Strauss; patent 139,121 was granted 20 May 1873.
Doubt beat: Pants tore at the same stress points no matter how they were sewn.
Reversal: The rivet patent created blue jeans.
Mechanism: A metal rivet spreads the pulling force over a stronger point instead of a thread seam.
Fit score: 7/10 (action 2, doubt 1, reversal 2, mechanism 1, evidence 1)
Competition: high (5+ Shorts) - only with a fresh angle (Davis, not Levi, had the idea).
Evidence: https://prologue.blogs.archives.gov/2010/05/20/forever-in-blue-jeansand-in-court/ · https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/levis-jeans-150-years-180982241/
Watch out: 1871 vs 1872 dates.
